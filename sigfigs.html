Significant Figures

Counting, rounding and computing with measured numbers
Abstract A measured number carries its precision in the way it is written: 2.5 m and 2.500 m are not the same measurement. The significant figures of a number are the digits that carry this information, and the rules below say how to count them, how to round, and how many to keep after multiplying, adding, taking a power, a root, a logarithm or an exponential, so that a result never claims more precision than its data. Tools apply the rules digit by digit and step by step, to rounding, to whole expressions and to molar masses; then twenty-two worked examples, from simple arithmetic to physics, chemistry and diving, and on to the propagation of uncertainties, which is what the rules approximate.
“The lack of mathematical culture is revealed nowhere so conspicuously as in meaningless precision in numerical computations.”— Carl Friedrich Gauss

2.1.1What the digits of a measurement say

Every measurement is uncertain. A ruler graduated in millimetres lets you read 23.4 mm, perhaps guess 23.45 mm, but never 23.4512 mm. The convention is to write all the digits you are sure of plus one more, the first uncertain one, and no others: those are the significant figures. Writing 23.4 mm says “between about 23.35 and 23.45”; writing 23.40 mm says “between about 23.395 and 23.405”, a measurement ten times more precise.

Significant figures concern precision (how finely a value is known), not accuracy (how close it is to the truth): a miscalibrated balance can give a precise but wrong 12.347 g. They are also a shorthand: the honest way to state precision is an explicit uncertainty, 23.40 ± 0.05 mm (§ 2.1.11). The rules exist so that a calculation keeps roughly the right number of digits without a full uncertainty analysis.

2.1.2Counting significant figures

Rule 1. Every nonzero digit is significant. 4.52 has 3; 7 has 1.
Rule 2. Zeros between nonzero digits (captive zeros) are significant. 1005 has 4; 3.08 has 3.
Rule 3. Leading zeros, before the first nonzero digit, are never significant: they only place the decimal point. 0.0045 has 2, the same as 4.5 × 10−3.
Rule 4. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant: someone measured them. 2.500 has 4; 0.0200 has 3.
Rule 5. Trailing zeros in a whole number written without a decimal point are ambiguous. 1500 has 2, 3 or 4: the writer must say which. A final decimal point marks them all significant (1500. has 4), and scientific notation removes all doubt.
Rule 6. In scientific notation, count the digits of the mantissa only; the power of ten carries no precision. 1.50 × 103 has 3; 1.5 × 103 has 2.
Rule 7. Exact numbers have infinitely many significant figures and never limit a result: counted objects (12 eggs), integers in formulas (the 2 in 2πr, the 12 in 12mv2), defined conversions (1 in = 2.54 cm exactly), and the constants fixed by the SI since 2019 (§ 2.1.7).

2.1.3Rounding

Rounding is how a full calculator value becomes a result with the right number of figures. The procedure is always the same.

  1. Find the last digit to keep. Count n significant figures from the first nonzero digit (to round to n figures), or go to the decimal place required (to round after a sum, rule 13).
  2. Look at the digit just after it, the first digit dropped, and, if it is a 5, at all the digits after that.
  3. Decide. Below 5: the kept digit stays. Above 5, or 5 followed by anything that is not zero: the kept digit goes up by one. Exactly 5 (nothing or only zeros after): a tie, rule 9.
  4. Carry. If the kept digit was a 9 and goes up, it becomes 0 and the digit before it goes up by one, as far as needed: 9.96 → 10.0.
  5. Drop the rest, and write the result so its figures are readable: keep zeros the rounding produced after the decimal point; replace dropped digits before the decimal point by a power of ten (rule 11).
Rule 8. If the first dropped digit is below 5, leave the last kept digit as it is: 3.1416 → 3.14. If it is above 5, or 5 followed by any nonzero digit, add one: 2.6789 → 2.68; 2.6751 → 2.68.
Rule 9. If the dropped part is exactly 5 (or 5000…), schools usually round up (2.65 → 2.7), away from zero for negative numbers (−2.65 → −2.7). Laboratories and the ISO 80000-1 standard prefer to round to the even digit (2.65 → 2.6, 2.75 → 2.8), so that ties do not push a long series of results upwards. Either is acceptable if applied consistently.
Rule 10. Round once, from the full number. Rounding 2.346 to two figures gives 2.3; rounding it first to 2.35 and then to 2.4 is wrong (double rounding): only the first dropped digit, here the 4, decides.
Rule 11. When rounding leaves trailing zeros in the units, write them in scientific notation: 4567 to two figures is 4.6 × 103, not 4600, which would be ambiguous. Keep the zeros a rounding produces after the decimal point: 9.998 to three figures is 10.0, not 10.

2.1.4Multiplication and division

Rule 12. The result has as many significant figures as the factor with the fewest. What matters is relative precision: a number known to 2 figures is uncertain by about 1 to 10 %, and no product can do better.

4.56 × 1.4 = 6.384 → 6.4 (2 figures, from 1.4)

0.02583.1416 = 0.008 212… → 0.008 21 (3 figures, from 0.0258)

The rule is a rough one: a number starting with 9 (9.8, uncertain by 1 %) is as precise as one starting with 1 and having one more digit (1.02, uncertain by 1 %). Some books therefore keep one extra figure when the least precise factor begins with 8 or 9, or when the result begins with 1. When it matters, compute the uncertainty (§ 2.1.11).

2.1.5Addition and subtraction

Rule 13. The result is rounded at the decimal place of the least precise term: the leftmost of the terms’ last significant places. What matters here is absolute precision, so it is the position of the last digit that counts, not the number of figures.

12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013 = 31.123 → 31.1 (tenths, from 18.0)

1.50 × 103 + 26.4 = 1526.4 → 1.53 × 103 (tens, from 1.50 × 103)

A sum can have more figures than its terms (9.7 + 0.6 = 10.3: two figures in, three out), and a difference can lose almost all of them: 1.0000 − 0.9998 = 0.0002 has one significant figure, though both terms had five. This loss in subtracting nearly equal numbers, cancellation, is the main way precision disappears in a calculation; rearrange the formula to avoid it when you can.

2.1.6Powers, roots, logarithms, exponentials and other functions

Rule 14. A power or a root keeps the significant figures of its base: (2.5)3 = 15.625 → 16; 30.0 = 5.477… → 5.48. (A square doubles the relative uncertainty and a square root halves it, but the rule of thumb ignores this.)
Rule 15. A logarithm has as many decimal places as its argument has significant figures. The digits before the decimal point only give the power of ten, so they are not counted: log10(2.5 × 10−4) = −3.602… → −3.60. That is why a pH of 3.60 corresponds to a concentration known to 2 figures. The same holds for ln.
Rule 16. Conversely, 10x (or ex) has as many significant figures as x has decimal places: 10−4.75 = 1.778… × 10−5 → 1.8 × 10−5.
Rule 17. For any other function (sin, cos, tan…), there is no reliable rule of digits. Evaluate the function at the value and at the value moved by one unit of its last digit, and keep the digits that do not change: sin(30.0°) = 0.5000, sin(30.1°) = 0.5015, so the result is 0.500, three figures; but cos(89.0°) = 0.0175 and cos(89.1°) = 0.0157, so it is 0.017 or even 0.02.

2.1.7Exact numbers and constants

Exact numbers never limit the significant figures of a result (rule 7). Besides counts and the integers of formulas, many constants are exact by definition. Since the revision of the SI in 2019, seven constants have fixed values, from which the units are defined:

So are the constants built from them, such as the gas constant R = NAk = 8.314 462 618… J·mol−1·K−1, and conventional values such as standard gravity gn = 9.806 65 m·s−2 and the standard atmosphere 101 325 Pa. In practice, use a constant with at least one or two more figures than your least precise datum, and it will not limit the result. Measured constants are not exact: the gravitational constant G = 6.674 30(15) × 10−11 m3·kg−1·s−2 is known to only about five figures. Neither are atomic weights, which carry their own number of figures (1.008, 15.999).

Conversions between units are exact when they are definitions (1 h = 3600 s, 1 L = 1 dm³, 1 bar = 105 Pa, 0 °C = 273.15 K). Adding 273.15 to a Celsius temperature is an addition, though, and rule 13 applies: 25 °C becomes 298 K, 25.0 °C becomes 298.2 K.

2.1.8Calculations in several steps

Rule 18. Do not round intermediate results. Keep every digit (in the calculator’s memory) or at least two guard digits beyond the significant ones, and round only the final answer. Rounding at every step adds a rounding error at every step: 13 rounded to 0.33, then multiplied by 3, gives 0.99 instead of 1.
Rule 19. Still, follow the significant figures at every step, applying to each operation its own rule (12 or 13), to know how many the final answer may keep. Parentheses first: in (5.25 + 3.1) × 2.000, the sum 8.35 is known only to the tenth, 8.4, so 2 figures; the product is 16.70, rounded to 2 figures: 17.

When a calculation mixes sums and products, the figures are decided operation by operation, not by counting over the whole formula: this is why a short subtraction inside a long product can ruin its precision (examples 6 and 17).

2.1.9Calculators that show the rules

The first evaluates a whole expression, operation by operation, the way you would by hand: every intermediate result is shown in full (it is kept unrounded for the next step, rule 18), with the number of significant figures it carries and the rule that decides it; only the final answer is rounded. Write a measured whole number with a decimal point (25.) or in scientific notation; whole numbers written without one, and powers of ten written 1e5, are taken as exact (counts, coefficients, exponents, unit conversions) while the box below is ticked. π and e are exact. Type / for a division: it is shown as a fraction.

Operations: + − × (or *) ÷ (or /) ^ and parentheses; functions sqrt( ), log( ) (base 10), ln( ), exp( ), pow10( ); constants pi, e. Numbers: 0.0250, 1.50e3, 2,5.

The second builds the molar mass of any chemical formula from the IUPAC atomic weights of the periodic table. Each count of atoms is exact, so multiplying by it keeps the significant figures of the atomic weight, not its decimal places; the sum is then rounded at the coarsest decimal place (rules 12 and 13).

Formulas such as H2O, Ca(OH)2, CuSO4·5H2O (or CuSO4.5H2O), K4[Fe(CN)6]. Atomic weights in brackets are mass numbers of radioactive elements, known only to the unit.

2.1.10Worked examples, from simple to complicated

Ordered from simple to complicated. Each shows the formula, then every intermediate result in full with the figures it carries and the rule that decides it; the answer is rounded once, at the end. Most can be replayed in the calculator of § 2.1.9.

Example 1 · Area of a sheet simple · product

A rectangle measures 12.5 cm by 3.2 cm. What is its area?

A = a × b
  1. 12.5 has 3 figures, 3.2 has 2. rule 1
  2. A = 12.5 × 3.2 = 40.0 cm2, in full.
  3. A product keeps the fewest figures: 2. rule 12
  4. 40 written plainly would be ambiguous: scientific notation. rule 11
Answer 4.0 × 101 cm2 (or 40. cm2, with a decimal point)

Example 2 · Total mass simple · sum

Three samples weigh 12.11 g, 18.0 g and 1.013 g, on three different balances. Total mass?

m = m1 + m2 + m3
  1. Last significant places: hundredths, tenths, thousandths. In a sum the places decide, not the number of figures. rule 13
  2. 12.11 + 18.0 + 1.013 = 31.123 g.
  3. Round at the coarsest place, the tenths of 18.0. rules 8, 13
Answer 31.1 g

Example 3 · Kinetic energy physics · exact factors

A ball of 2.50 kg moves at 3.0 m·s−1. Its kinetic energy?

Ek = 12 mv2
  1. The 12 and the exponent 2 are exact: they do not limit anything. rule 7
  2. v2 = 9.0 m2·s−2: 2 figures, those of 3.0. rule 14
  3. Ek = 12 × 2.50 × 9.0 = 11.25 J.
  4. The measured factors have 3 and 2 figures: 2. rule 12
Answer 11 J

Example 4 · Speed of a falling stone physics · root

A stone falls 1.25 m (g = 9.81 m·s−2). Its speed at the bottom, air resistance neglected?

v = 2gh
  1. 2gh = 2 × 9.81 × 1.25 = 24.525 m2·s−2: 3 figures; the 2 is exact. rules 7, 12
  2. v = 24.525 = 4.9523 m·s−1: a root keeps the 3 figures. rule 14
Answer 4.95 m·s−1

Example 5 · g from a pendulum physics · a formula with π

A pendulum of length 0.750 m has a period of 1.74 s. Since T = 2πLg:

g = 4π2LT2
  1. 4 and the exponent 2 are exact; π is taken with all the calculator’s digits. rule 7
  2. Numerator: 4π2 × 0.750 = 29.609 m, 3 figures (from 0.750). rule 12
  3. Denominator: 1.742 = 3.0276 s2, 3 figures, kept unrounded. rules 14, 18
  4. g = 29.6093.0276 = 9.7796 m·s−2: 3 figures. rule 12
Answer 9.78 m·s−2 (its uncertainty is computed in example 19)

Example 6 · Density by water displacement physics · a subtraction loses a figure

A pebble of 25.47 g is dropped into a graduated cylinder: the water rises from 12.3 mL to 21.8 mL. Its density?

ρ = mV2 − V1
  1. Denominator first: 21.8 − 12.3 = 9.5 mL, rounded at the tenths. rule 13
  2. 9.5 has only 2 figures, though both readings had 3: the subtraction lost one.
  3. ρ = 25.479.5 = 2.6811 g·mL−1: 4 and 2 figures, so 2. rule 12
Answer 2.7 g·mL−1: the 4 figures of the balance are wasted; to do better, measure the volume better.

Example 7 · Molar mass of water chemistry · exact counts

Atomic weights: H 1.008, O 15.999 g·mol−1. Molar mass of H2O?

M(H2O) = 2 M(H) + M(O)
  1. The 2 of H2 is a count: exact. 2 × 1.008 = 2.016, still 4 figures, last place the thousandths. rules 7, 12
  2. 2.016 + 15.999 = 18.015: both terms end at the thousandths. rule 13
Answer 18.015 g·mol−1, 5 figures, more than either atomic weight: a sum can gain figures.

Example 8 · Molar mass of glucose chemistry · when an exact count moves the last place

Glucose is C6H12O6. Atomic weights: C 12.011, H 1.008, O 15.999 g·mol−1.

M = 6 M(C) + 12 M(H) + 6 M(O)
  1. 6 × 12.011 = 72.066: 5 figures (from 12.011), last place the thousandths. rules 7, 12
  2. 12 × 1.008 = 12.096: 1.008 has only 4 figures, so the product keeps 4: 12.10, last place the hundredths. Multiplying by an exact 12 multiplies the uncertainty of 1.008 (±0.001) by 12 too: ±0.012, a hundredth. rules 7, 12
  3. 6 × 15.999 = 95.994: 5 figures, the thousandths. rule 12
  4. Sum, with the full values: 72.066 + 12.096 + 95.994 = 180.156. rule 18
  5. The coarsest last place is the hundredths, from the hydrogen term: round there. rules 8, 13
Answer 180.16 g·mol−1, not 180.156: the twelve hydrogens limit the result.

Example 9 · Molar mass of a hydrate chemistry · grouping does not change the answer

Copper sulfate pentahydrate is CuSO4·5H2O. Atomic weights: Cu 63.546, S 32.06, O 15.999, H 1.008 g·mol−1.

M = M(Cu) + M(S) + 4 M(O) + 5 M(H2O)
  1. M(H2O) = 2 × 1.008 + 15.999 = 18.015, the thousandths (example 7). rule 13
  2. 5 × 18.015 = 90.075: 5 figures, the thousandths. rule 12
  3. 4 × 15.999 = 63.996: 5 figures, the thousandths. rule 12
  4. 63.546 + 32.06 + 63.996 + 90.075 = 249.677: sulfur, 32.06, is known only to the hundredths. rule 13
  5. Counting atom by atom instead, 9 O and 10 H: 9 × 15.999 = 143.99 (hundredths) and 10 × 1.008 = 10.08 (hundredths): the same 249.677, the same last place.
Answer 249.68 g·mol−1

Example 10 · Preparing a solution chemistry · two quotients

3.65 g of NaCl (M = 58.44 g·mol−1) is dissolved and made up to 250.0 mL in a volumetric flask. Amount and concentration?

n = mM ,   c = nV
  1. n = 3.6558.44 = 0.062 457 mol: 3 figures, reported 0.0625 mol, kept in full. rules 12, 18
  2. V = 250.0 mL = 0.2500 L: changing the unit does not change the figures (4).
  3. c = 0.062 4570.2500 = 0.249 83 mol·L−1: 3 figures, from the mass. rule 12
Answer n = 0.0625 mol; c = 0.250 mol·L−1: the final zero is significant and must be written.

Example 11 · A titration chemistry · a difference inside a product

25.00 mL of hydrochloric acid (pipette) is titrated with sodium hydroxide at 0.1050 mol·L−1. The burette reads 0.35 mL before and 23.85 mL at the end point. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O, one to one (exact).

cHCl = cNaOH (Vend − Vstart)VHCl
  1. Volume delivered: 23.85 − 0.35 = 23.50 mL, to the hundredths: 4 figures. Here the subtraction loses nothing, the readings being far apart. rule 13
  2. Numerator: 0.1050 × 23.50 = 2.4675 mmol·…, 4 and 4 figures: 4. rule 12
  3. cHCl = 0.1050 × 23.5025.00 = 0.098 70 mol·L−1: 4 figures. The millilitres cancel; no conversion is needed. rule 12
Answer 0.098 70 mol·L−1, written with its final zero.

Example 12 · pH and back chemistry · logarithm, antilogarithm

(a) [H3O+] = 2.5 × 10−4 mol·L−1: pH? (b) A buffer has pH 4.75: [H3O+]?

pH = −log10 [H3O+] ,   [H3O+] = 10−pH
  1. (a) −log10(2.5 × 10−4) = 3.6021: 2 figures give 2 decimal places; the 3 only carries the power of ten. rule 15
  2. (b) 10−4.75 = 1.7783 × 10−5: 2 decimal places give 2 figures. rule 16
Answer (a) pH = 3.60; (b) [H3O+] = 1.8 × 10−5 mol·L−1

Example 13 · Gas in a flask chemistry · a temperature conversion inside a quotient

2.50 L of gas at 101.3 kPa and 25 °C. Amount of gas?

n = pVRT
  1. T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K, rounded at the units of 25: 298 K, 3 figures. 273.15 is exact, 25 is measured. rule 13
  2. SI units: p = 1.013 × 105 Pa, V = 2.50 × 10−3 m3; R = 8.314 J·mol−1·K−1, more figures than the data. § 2.1.7
  3. n = 1.013 × 105 × 2.50 × 10−38.314 × 298.15 = 253.252478.8 = 0.102 17 mol. rule 18
  4. Fewest figures: 3, from 2.50 and from the temperature. rule 12
Answer 0.102 mol

Example 14 · Density of air chemistry · four data, one constant

Air (M = 28.96 g·mol−1) at 98.6 kPa and 18.5 °C. Its density, from the ideal gas law?

ρ = pMRT
  1. T = 18.5 + 273.15 = 291.65, rounded at the tenths: 291.7 K, 4 figures. rule 13
  2. M = 0.028 96 kg·mol−1, 4 figures; R = 8.314 462 618 J·mol−1·K−1, exact. § 2.1.7
  3. ρ = 98 600 × 0.028 968.314 462 618 × 291.65 = 2855.52424.9 = 1.1775 kg·m−3.
  4. Figures: 3 (pressure), 4, 4: the pressure decides. rule 12
Answer 1.18 kg·m−3

Example 15 · Energy of a photon physics · exact SI constants

A green laser emits at 532 nm. Energy of one photon, in joules and in electronvolts?

E = hcλ
  1. h, c and e are exact since 2019: only λ limits the result, to 3 figures. rule 7, § 2.1.7
  2. E = 6.626 070 15 × 10−34 × 299 792 458532 × 10−9 = 3.7339 × 10−19 J.
  3. In eV: 3.7339 × 10−191.602 176 634 × 10−19 = 2.3305 eV. rule 12
Answer 3.73 × 10−19 J = 2.33 eV

Example 16 · Oxygen at depth diving · product, sum, product

A diver breathes Nitrox 32 (oxygen fraction 0.32) at 33.5 m in seawater (ρ = 1025 kg·m−3, g = 9.81 m·s−2), under 1.013 bar of atmosphere. Partial pressure of oxygen?

pO2 = fO2 × (ρgh105 + patm)
  1. Water: 1025 × 9.81 × 33.5105 = 3.368 51 bar (105 Pa per bar, exact): 3 figures, so to the hundredths. rule 12
  2. Absolute: 3.368 51 + 1.013 = 4.381 51 bar, rounded at the hundredths of the water term: 4.38 bar. rule 13
  3. pO2 = 0.32 × 4.381 51 = 1.4021 bar: 2 figures, from 0.32. rule 12
Answer pO2 = 1.4 bar, the usual working limit. To know whether it is 1.38 or 1.42, the gas must be analysed to more than two figures.

Example 17 · Calorimetry physics · two subtractions in a quotient

A metal block of 50.0 g at 98.7 °C is dropped into 150.0 g of water at 22.5 °C (cw = 4.184 J·g−1·K−1); both end at 25.8 °C. Specific heat of the metal?

cm = mw cw (Tf − Tw)mm (Tm − Tf)
  1. Heat gained by the water equals heat lost by the metal; solve for cm.
  2. Warming of the water: 25.8 − 22.5 = 3.3 K, to the tenths: only 2 figures. rule 13
  3. Cooling of the metal: 98.7 − 25.8 = 72.9 K: 3 figures. rule 13
  4. Numerator: 150.0 × 4.184 × 3.3 = 2071.1 J: 4, 4 and 2 figures, so 2. rule 12
  5. Denominator: 50.0 × 72.9 = 3645 g·K: 3 figures. rule 12
  6. cm = 2071.13645 = 0.568 20 J·g−1·K−1: 2 figures. rule 12
Answer 0.57 J·g−1·K−1. The small temperature rise of the water, known to 2 figures, limits everything: a better thermometer would help more than a better balance.

Example 18 · Burning propane chemistry · a chain of five steps, and the price of rounding too early

25.0 g of propane burns completely: C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O. What mass of CO2 forms? (C 12.011, H 1.008, O 15.999 g·mol−1)

m(CO2) = 3 × m(C3H8)M(C3H8) × M(CO2)
  1. M(C3H8) = 3 × 12.011 + 8 × 1.008 = 36.033 + 8.064 = 44.097 g·mol−1, to the thousandths: 5 figures. rules 7, 12, 13
  2. M(CO2) = 12.011 + 2 × 15.999 = 44.009 g·mol−1: 5 figures. rule 13
  3. n(C3H8) = 25.044.097 = 0.566 93 mol: 3 figures, kept in full. rules 12, 18
  4. n(CO2) = 3 × 0.566 93 = 1.700 80 mol: the 3 of the equation is exact, 3 figures remain. rule 7
  5. m(CO2) = 1.700 80 × 44.009 = 74.850 g: 3 figures. rule 12
  6. Had each step been rounded (0.567, then 1.70, then 1.70 × 44.0), the answer would have been 74.8 g: wrong in its last figure. rule 18
Answer 74.9 g of CO2

Example 19 · The pendulum, with its uncertainty complicated · propagation

In example 5, L = 0.750 ± 0.002 m and T = 1.74 ± 0.01 s. How precise is g = 9.7796 m·s−2 really?

u(g)g = (u(L)L)2 + (2 u(T)T)2
  1. For g ∝ LT−2, relative uncertainties combine in quadrature, each multiplied by its exponent. § 2.1.11
  2. 0.0020.750 = 0.002 67 ;   2 × 0.011.74 = 0.011 49
  3. u(g)g = 0.002 672 + 0.011 492 = 0.0118 ;   u(g) = 9.7796 × 0.0118 = 0.115 m·s−2
  4. Round the uncertainty to 2 figures, 0.12, and the value to the same decimal place. § 2.1.11
Answer g = 9.78 ± 0.12 m·s−2. The 3 figures of example 5 were right; the period, squared, dominates the uncertainty.

Example 20 · Radioactive decay complicated · a measured exponent

5.00 g of iodine-131 (half-life T = 8.02 days) decays for 12.0 days. How much is left?

m = m0 (12)tT
  1. Exponent: 12.08.02 = 1.4963: 3 figures. rule 12
  2. (12)1.4963 = 101.4963 × log10 0.5 = 10−0.4504: this exponent has 3 figures, so 3 decimal places, so the power has 3 figures. rule 16
  3. 10−0.4504 = 0.354 47 ;   m = 5.00 × 0.354 47 = 1.7724 g: 3 figures. rule 12
Answer 1.77 g

Example 21 · Sound level complicated · logarithm of a ratio

A sound has an intensity of 3.2 × 10−5 W·m−2; the reference I0 = 10−12 W·m−2 is a definition, exact. Its level?

L = 10 log10 II0
  1. 3.2 × 10−510−12 = 3.2 × 107: 2 figures. rule 12
  2. log10(3.2 × 107) = 7.5051: 2 decimal places, 7.51. rule 15
  3. × 10, exact: 75.051, the decimal place moves with it: 75.1 dB. rule 7
Answer 75.1 dB: 3 figures from a 2-figure intensity; after a logarithm, count decimals, not figures.

Example 22 · Average of repeated measurements complicated · statistics

Five measurements of g: 9.81, 9.79, 9.83, 9.80, 9.82 m·s−2. Best value?

ḡ = Σ giN ,   u(ḡ) = sN
  1. Sum: 49.05, to the hundredths; mean: 49.055 = 9.810, the 5 being a count, exact. rules 7, 13
  2. Standard deviation s = 0.016 m·s−2: the scatter of one measurement.
  3. Standard error of the mean: 0.0165 = 0.0071 m·s−2: averaging N measurements shrinks the uncertainty by N, so the mean may keep a digit no single measurement has. § 2.1.11
Answer g = 9.810 ± 0.007 m·s−2

2.1.11Beyond the rules: uncertainties

The rules of significant figures approximate the propagation of uncertainties, the method of metrology (the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement, GUM). Each datum is given with a standard uncertainty u, and for independent data:

u(a ± b) = u(a)2 + u(b)2 sums: absolute uncertainties combine (rule 13)
u(ab)ab = u(ab)ab = (u(a)a)2 + (u(b)b)2 products and quotients: relative uncertainties combine (rule 12)
u(an)an = |n| u(a)a      u(f) = |f ′(a)| u(a) powers and any function

The last line explains rules 15 and 16: u(log10 a) = 0.434 u(a)a, an absolute uncertainty on the logarithm set by the relative uncertainty of a. To report a result: round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures (two when its first digit is 1 or 2), then round the value to the same decimal place: 9.7796 ± 0.1154 becomes 9.78 ± 0.12, and 0.249 83 ± 0.000 43 becomes 0.2498 ± 0.0004. When the uncertainty is known, it decides the digits, not the rules.

2.1.12Common mistakes

  • Copying the calculator. 25.479.5 = 2.681 052 631 6 is not a density of ten figures; it is 2.7 g·mL−1.
  • Dropping significant zeros. 0.250 mol·L−1 is not 0.25 mol·L−1: the zero says the third figure was measured.
  • Rounding along the way. Round once, at the end (rule 18); round from the full number, never from a rounded one (rule 10).
  • Counting figures in a sum. In a sum, the decimal place decides (rule 13): 100.1 + 0.0035 = 100.1, not 100.10.
  • Counting figures in a logarithm. pH 7.0 has one significant figure in the concentration it stands for (rule 15).
  • Letting exact numbers limit the result. The 2 of 2πr, the 12 of a dozen, 1 in = 2.54 cm: all exact (rule 7).
  • Writing ambiguous zeros. 4600 with two figures is 4.6 × 103 (rule 11).
  • Forgetting the units. A significant figure belongs to a quantity with its unit: 0.002 50 kg = 2.50 g, 3 figures either way.
