GUE Standard Gas Mixes

What they are, why GUE uses them, and how
Abstract Global Underwater Explorers (GUE) does not let each diver pick a “best mix” for every dive. It uses a short list of standard mixes, each covering a range of depths, plus a few standard decompression gases switched at fixed depths. This page explains the rules the mixes are built on, why a whole team breathes the same gases, and how the mixes are used on a dive, then sums them up in one table.

Reading a mix

A mix is named by its oxygen and helium percentages, and the rest is nitrogen. EAN32 (Nitrox 32) is 32% oxygen and 68% nitrogen. Trimix 21/35 is 21% oxygen, 35% helium and 44% nitrogen. Helium is added to make the gas less narcotic and less dense at depth. Oxygen is reduced on deeper mixes so that its partial pressure stays safe.

The rules behind the mixes

pO₂ = FO₂ (d/10 + 1)(1) END = (d + 10)(1 − FHe) − 10(2) d = depth (m), F = gas fraction; END counts oxygen as narcotic, as GUE does
  • Oxygen. During the working part of the dive the oxygen partial pressure stays around 1.2–1.3 bar, at most 1.4 bar. During decompression it is raised to 1.4 bar on deep stops and 1.6 bar at the 6 m stop, to speed up off-gassing while the diver is resting.
  • Narcosis. The equivalent narcotic depth (END) stays at about 30 m or less. GUE counts oxygen as narcotic, so only helium lowers the END (equation 2). At 30 m a diver can still think clearly and solve problems.
  • Gas density. Dense gas is harder to breathe and builds up carbon dioxide. The equivalent air density depth (EADD), the depth at which air would be as dense as the mix, stays close to 30 m on the standard mixes up to 60 m.
  • No deep air. Below 30 m GUE uses helium mixes, never air.

Why standard gases

  • Team. Every diver in the team breathes the same gas. A donated gas is the planned gas at the planned depth, and the whole team shares one decompression schedule and one maximum depth.
  • Familiarity. Diving the same few gases again and again, divers learn their no-stop times, decompression and oxygen exposure, and can plan with simple rules of thumb.
  • Fewer choices, fewer errors. There is no debate between 30% and 28%: the depth picks the gas. Fewer numbers means fewer mistakes in planning, labelling and analysis.
  • Margin. Each mix covers a range of depths, so a few metres beyond the plan does not put the diver in serious trouble.
  • Blending. The deep trimixes are made by adding helium and topping up with EAN32: 35% helium and 65% EAN32 give 20.8% oxygen, 21/35; the same recipe, with 45, 55, 65 or 70% helium, gives about 18/45, 15/55, 12/65 and 10/70. Leftover helium is used up, and fill stations learn a few recipes.

How they are used

  • Bottom gas is carried in the back-mounted twinset and chosen from the deepest planned depth: EAN32 to 30 m, then 21/35 to 45 m and 18/45 to 60 m, and deeper trimixes beyond. Trimix 30/30 is an alternative to EAN32 down to 30 m, with less narcosis and lower density.
  • Decompression gas is carried in stage cylinders, each labelled with its gas and maximum depth and analysed before the dive. On a Tech 1 dive the single decompression gas is EAN50, switched at 21 m. Deeper dives add oxygen at 6 m and, for long decompressions, trimix 35/25 from 36 m.
  • Switching. The team switches gas together at the planned depth, confirming the cylinder and its label for each diver before breathing from it.
  • Travel gas. With 15/55 and deeper mixes the bottom gas is hypoxic: it has too little oxygen to breathe at the surface. The diver descends and ascends on 21/35 as a travel gas, which is also used for decompression from 57 m.
  • Rebreathers. The same mixes are used as diluents on closed-circuit rebreathers, and as bailout gas.
